Payment status: captured means the money was taken. It can still change: a refund comes later. Not Qafila: a payment provider charges Khalid's card.
Dark store: a store built for delivery only, a small warehouse with no shoppers, only pickers. Qafila has 12, so every customer is close to one.
SLA (service level agreement): the promise a team is measured against. Qafila's is 30 minutes; this order took 19 minutes from order to door.
Build this slide one step per key press; each press adds the next step of order 5043883. Step 2: real payment pay_9040078, 89.50 SAR by card, captured 20 seconds after the order. Qafila does not hold card details; the payment provider does and tells Qafila the result. Over two months 1,582 payment attempts failed; some captured payments were refunded later. Checking that the amount paid matches the order is reconciliation. Step 3: the order goes to the nearest dark store, RUH-05 Al Naseem; a picker collects the cucumbers and the coffee; a courier is assigned at 01:36 and collects the bag at 01:46. The dark store holds stock, so it is also where suppliers deliver. Step 4: the courier app reports each step: assigned 01:36, picked up 01:46, out for delivery 01:48, delivered 01:54:55. From the order at 01:35:37 that is 19.3 minutes, inside the 30-minute promise. Operations lives on this number: which stores and which hours miss it?

Personal data: anything that identifies a person, like a name, a mobile or a national ID. The PDPL protects it.
Customers change after they sign up: profiles move, reviews arrive, some ask to be forgotten.
Khalid's own story is short: signed up 26 September 12:51, first order 28 September. Other customers show the rest: in September 1,541 signed up, 4,458 profile edits happened and 159 customers asked to be erased (counted from the change feed in Lab 1.4). Reviews come in Arabic and English. PDPL is Saudi Arabia's Personal Data Protection Law: it limits who may see personal data and gives people the right to have it destroyed.

The order lane again; now look under it. The app database records the order, the payment provider records the payment, the courier app sends an event for each delivery step. State or events: most sources keep only the state. History survives only where someone records events.
The same order, as the app database and the courier app record it. The app database keeps one row per order, and every new status overwrote the old one: it now says delivered, nothing more. The courier app is the opposite: each step is a new event, kept in time order. Both are normal. Day 1 copies state and turns its changes into events (CDC, D1_4); Day 3 processes events as they arrive (D3_3).

Yes: the picked_up event, 01:46. The order row now says only "delivered", and the payment knows only money. The current state cannot tell you about the past; only a record of what happened can.
Allow 30 seconds; ask the room to say why the other two cannot answer it. C is right: the picked_up event at 01:46. Data science is the odd one out: not fresh, but long. It needs months of history.
Freshness: how old an answer may be before it is useless. It decides between a nightly batch and a live stream.
Walk the axis left to right. A fraud check must answer before the payment is captured: seconds. Operations must see orders about to miss 30 minutes while there is still time to act: minutes. Finance closes yesterday's numbers the next morning, exact. Purchasing reacts when the weekly price lists arrive. Data science needs long history rather than fresh data. A batch collects records and processes them on a schedule; a stream processes each event as it arrives. Most of the week is batch; Day 3 adds streaming.
