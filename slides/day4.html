Takeaway. A snapshot is one complete version of the table. Atomic commit: readers see the old version whole or the new one whole.
Snapshot numbers on the board are illustrative. The job prepares its new files where nobody is looking, then publishes them in one step. A query that started on 41 finishes on 41. PDPL and NDMO, from a data engineer’s seat

The Personal Data Protection Law: in force since September 2023, fully enforced since September 2024. SDAIA is the regulator.

The rule saysThe pipeline does
know what personal data you holdPII discovery, a catalog with a classification per column
collect and show only what the purpose needspseudonyms for analysts, masked views per role, gold without identifiers
honour data subjects’ rights: access, correction, erasurea deletion that reaches every copy, verified
keep transfers outside the Kingdom under controlstorage region, backups and LLM endpoints inside approved locations
be able to show itlineage, access logs, retention jobs
Takeaway. Each principle becomes a feature you can build, test and show to an auditor.
PDPL covers lawful purpose, data minimization, data subjects' rights (access, correction, destruction), breach notification and conditions on transfers outside the Kingdom. The NDMO (National Data Management Office) standards add governance roles, a catalog, and the classification levels on the next board. Your data protection officer and your organization's policies decide what applies. Today's lab covers the first two rows.

A regex finds too much

Ten digits starting with 1 or 2 look like a national ID. So do the couriers’ tracking numbers.

\b[12]\d{9}\b · in the 400 notes83 candidates
… that pass the Luhn check77 national IDs
… that don’t6 tracking numbers

A check digit tells a real ID from a random 10-digit number.

IBANs have one too: ISO 13616, mod 97.

Takeaway. A pattern finds candidates, and a checksum confirms them. Fewer false alarms, more trust in the inventory.
Real counts from Lab 4.4 step 2: 83 candidates, 77 pass, 6 are tracking numbers. Saudi IDs and Iqama numbers end with a Luhn check digit: from the right, double every second digit, subtract 9 from anything above 9, add everything up, and the total divides by 10. Khalid's sums to 50. Some personal data has no pattern at all, such as names: declare it in the catalog instead.

A plain hash re-identifies everyone

Lab 4.4’s attacker holds 500 leaked IDs and the “anonymous” hashed table.

sha256("1278681299")anyone can compute thised064fa5…
hmac(key, "1278681299")only whoever holds the key6fb6a0bd…
500 of 500re-identified from plain hashes
0 of 500without the key
58people with two accounts: still found, from tokens alone

The key lives in a secret manager. Rotate it and every token changes.

Takeaway. Pseudonymize: swap an ID for a token. A keyed hash (HMAC) makes one nobody can recompute without the key.
Real results from Lab 4.4 step 4: 7,442 accounts belong to 7,384 people. With plain SHA-256 the attacker matches all 500 leaked IDs; with HMAC-SHA256 and a secret key, none. The tokens still find the 58 people with two accounts. Pseudonymized data is still personal data under PDPL: whoever holds the key can match it. True anonymization is a much higher bar.
